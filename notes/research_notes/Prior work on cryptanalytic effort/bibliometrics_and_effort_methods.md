# Bibliometrics of cryptography and methods for estimating research effort from publications

Scope: prior work to cite for the 2026 study's methods (LLM two-pass classifier, fractional paper counts per assumption class, DBLP author resolution, output-share research-FTE model, career-stage research fractions) and a check for comparable cryptanalysis bibliometrics. Research date: 2026-10-09.

How entries were verified: each bibliographic entry (authors, title, venue, volume/pages, year) was checked against the Crossref REST API (`api.crossref.org/works/<DOI>`) and/or OpenAlex, the publisher page, or the arXiv abstract page. The verifying URL is the DOI link given in each entry. Summaries come from the abstract (via OpenAlex, Semantic Scholar, or the publisher) or from the full text where noted. Citation counts are Crossref `is-referenced-by-count` values on 2026-10-09 and are a rough indicator of standing only.

## Q1. Has anyone done bibliometrics specifically of cryptanalysis, or of attacks per assumption?

### Takeaway
I found no prior study that counts cryptanalytic papers or researchers per hardness assumption, or that converts cryptanalysis output into person-years. The closest prior work is (a) general scientometric profiles of cryptography (Pal 2015, covering the Journal of Cryptology 2001–2010), (b) Scopus/WoS bibliometric mappings of post-quantum cryptography, which are dominated by implementation and IoT work and are not about attacks, and (c) one bibliometric analysis of a single attack family, lattice-based cryptanalysis of RSA (Zheng & Kang 2024). The one quantitative "effort to assurance" model is Brown's (2019) Poisson model, which is theoretical and does not measure effort. The study appears to be the first per-assumption measurement of cryptanalytic effort.

### Cited Findings

**Comparable / closest studies (cryptography bibliometrics)**

- **Pal, J. K. (2015). Scientometric dimensions of cryptographic research. *Scientometrics*, 105(1), 179–202. doi:10.1007/s11192-015-1661-z.** Verified: Crossref and the ISI Kolkata repository record (https://digitalcommons.isical.ac.in/journal-articles/2285). *Summary:* A scientometric profile of the Journal of Cryptology's output for 2001–2010. About 25% of papers are single-authored, the mean is 2.4 authors per paper, the degree of collaboration is 0.74, author productivity fits Lotka's law only partially (exponent about 2.35), about 56% of collaborations cross national borders, and the USA and Israel dominate. "Cryptanalysis" and "elliptic curve" are among the most frequent keywords. *Relevance:* the only cryptography-wide scientometric paper found in a core bibliometrics journal (comparable study, but one journal, no per-assumption split, no effort conversion). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11192-015-1661-z); [repository record](https://digitalcommons.isical.ac.in/journal-articles/2285)

- **Zheng, M., & Kang, H. (2024). Lattice-based cryptanalysis of RSA-type cryptosystems: a bibliometric analysis. *Cybersecurity*, 7(1), 74. doi:10.1186/s42400-024-00289-7.** Verified: Crossref and OpenAlex. *Summary:* A Scopus-based bibliometric analysis, using CiteSpace and VOSviewer, of publications on lattice (Coppersmith-type) attacks on RSA variants. It covers publication trends, country, institution and author collaboration networks, co-citation clusters, and keyword hotspots. Per the paper's tables, China leads in publications (102, 25.5%), France is second (72), and the most prolific authors are Nitaj, Kunihiro, and Sarkar. *Relevance:* the closest precedent for bibliometrics of one cryptanalytic attack line. Springer. doi:10.1007/978-3-642-15464-5_24; arXiv:1012.5396.** Verified: Crossref and arXiv metadata. *Summary:* Uses DBLP to compare CS sub-communities, each defined by top venues, on productivity, career length, and collaboration. **Effort-to-assurance modelling (context, not bibliometric)**

- **Brown, D. R. L. (2019). An optimist's Poisson model of cryptanalysis. *IACR Cryptology ePrint Archive*, Paper 2019/1465.** Verified: ePrint page. *Summary:* Models the discovery of attacks as a Poisson arrival process to turn analysis effort into numerical security-assurance estimates. It concludes that more independent cryptanalytic effort increases assurance, but the result depends heavily on optimistic assumptions. *Relevance:* the closest theoretical framing for why measured cryptanalytic effort per assumption matters. Good to cite in the motivation. — [Source](https://eprint.iacr.org/2019/1465)

### Inferences
- Existing crypto bibliometrics either profile one venue (Pal) or map "PQC" as a keyword topic (Hasija et al.; Hanafi & Ali). Keyword topic maps conflate QKD, implementation, and attacks. None classifies papers by the hardness assumption they attack, and none estimates person-years. The study can reasonably claim novelty on (i) per-assumption classification of cryptanalysis and (ii) conversion to research-FTE. The claim should be worded as "we are not aware of" rather than as an absolute.
- Biryukov & Dong's finding that crypto has small teams and weakly clustered coauthor networks agrees with Pal's 2.4 authors per paper. Both support the study's choice of author-fractional credit: small teams mean fractional and full counts diverge less than in lab sciences.

- Not found (searched): any topic-model or trend analysis of the full IACR ePrint corpus; any co-authorship-network study restricted to IACR venues; any IACR-commissioned publication-statistics report. Searches for "IACR bibliometric," "ePrint topic modeling," and "CRYPTO EUROCRYPT scientometric" returned nothing on point.
- A 2013 WoS-based cryptography scientometric study (Baskaran, 2000–2011, 6,610 records) is cited by Hasija et al. but could not be located or verified. It is listed as unverified.
- **Unverified (not opened):** Gupta, B. & Rana, S., "GAUGE: A Formal Framework for Measuring Cryptographic Security under Heterogeneous Adversary Cost Models," arXiv:2609.17281 (submitted 2026-09-15). The arXiv abstract page was fetched and confirms the title, authors, and date. It is a very recent unrefereed preprint that reportedly includes a 25-year dataset of assumption breaks and a "lattice-sieving cost drift" figure. Only the abstract was read, so its content claims are unverified. It is possibly relevant as a concurrent "attacks per assumption over time" dataset. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.17281)

## Q2. What are standard, citable methods and parameters for converting publications to person-years or FTE, and what are typical papers-per-FTE-year values?

### Takeaway
The standard practice in evaluative bibliometrics is author-level fractional counting (credit 1/n per author), recommended by Waltman & van Eck (2015) and formalised by Gauffriau et al. (2007), with productivity defined as fractional output per unit of labour input (Abramo & D'Angelo's FSS divides by years worked and salary). The reverse step, converting output shares into time, rests on the OECD Frascati Manual's definition of FTE and on time-use surveys showing that faculty spend well under half their time on research. I found no canonical "papers per FTE-year" constant for mathematics or CS. Field publication rates exist (D'Angelo & Abramo 2015, 192 Italian fields), but the study's per-paper priors of 1–2 person-years are assumptions rather than literature values and should be presented as such.

### Cited Findings

**Fractional counting (the method used for class credit)**

- **Waltman, L., & van Eck, N. J. (2015). Field-normalized citation impact indicators and the choice of an appropriate counting method. *Journal of Informetrics*, 9(4), 872–894. doi:10.1016/j.joi.2015.08.001** (arXiv:1501.04431). Verified: Crossref. *Summary:* Argues on theoretical and empirical grounds that properly field-normalized results require fractional rather than full counting, and recommends author-level or address-level fractional counting. *Relevance:* the method used, the standard citation for 1/n author credit. 246 citations. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joi.2015.08.001)

- **Gauffriau, M., Larsen, P. O., Maye, I., Roulin-Perriard, A., & von Ins, M. (2007). Publication, cooperation and productivity measures in scientific research. *Scientometrics*, 73(2), 175–214. doi:10.1007/s11192-007-1800-2.** Verified: Crossref. *Summary:* A systematic taxonomy of counting methods (whole, complete-normalized/fractional, straight, and others) and how the choice changes publication, cooperation, and productivity measures. *Relevance:* a terminology reference for naming our counting scheme precisely ("complete-normalized" counting at the author level). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11192-007-1800-2)

- **Sivertsen, G., Rousseau, R., & Zhang, L. (2019). Measuring scientific contributions with modified fractional counting. *Journal of Informetrics*, 13(2), 679–694. doi:10.1016/j.joi.2019.03.010.** Verified: Crossref and OpenAlex. *Summary:* Proposes modified fractional counting (MFC), a parameterised family that damps the extreme differences between scientists who publish alone or in small groups and those in large teams. It makes average contributions comparable across fields (tested on Norwegian universities). *Relevance:* a sensitivity-analysis option. Crypto's small teams make the full and fractional results close, but MFC is the citable alternative if reviewers object to 1/n. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joi.2019.03.010)

**Productivity = output per labour input**

- **Abramo, G., & D'Angelo, C. A. (2014). How do you define and measure research productivity? *Scientometrics*, 101(2), 1129–1144. doi:10.1007/s11192-014-1269-8** (arXiv:1810.12830). Verified: Crossref. Formula read from the arXiv full text. *Summary:* Defines individual Fractional Scientific Strength as FSS_R = (1/w_R)(1/t) Σ_i (c_i / c̄) f_i, where t is years of work in the period, f_i is the researcher's fractional contribution (1/n authors in alphabetical-order fields), c_i/c̄ is field-normalized citations, and w_R is salary. If salary is unknown, they say to at least compare researchers of the same rank. *Relevance:* the method used, the canonical "fractional output per unit of labour per year" definition. Our share_c(y) is the inverse use: output share as a proxy for labour share. Both rest on fractional credit per year worked. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11192-014-1269-8); [arXiv full text](https://arxiv.org/abs/1810.12830)

- **D'Angelo, C. A., & Abramo, G. (2015). Publication rates in 192 research fields of the hard sciences. In *Proceedings of the 15th International Society of Scientometrics and Informetrics Conference (ISSI 2015)*, Istanbul, pp. 909–919.** Verified: CNR IRIS institutional record (https://iris.cnr.it/handle/20.500.14243/351686) for title, authors, and year. The venue and pages come from a citing paper's reference list (arXiv:1905.02464), so they are not checked against the proceedings themselves. *Summary:* Measures annual full and fractional publication rates for Italian academics in each of 192 hard-science fields (the Italian SDS classification, which includes mathematics and computer-science fields) and proposes them as field scaling factors. *Relevance:* the best available source for field-specific papers per researcher-year. The values for MAT/* and INF/01 would calibrate our 1 pub/year floor and the per-paper priors. Specific numbers not extracted (see Gaps). — [Source](https://iris.cnr.it/handle/20.500.14243/351686)

- **Abramo, G., D'Angelo, C. A., & Di Costa, F. (2011). Research productivity: are higher academic ranks more productive than lower ones? *Scientometrics*, 88(3), 915–928. doi:10.1007/s11192-011-0426-6.** Verified: Crossref and Semantic Scholar. *Summary:* Uses bibliometric data on all Italian hard-science university researchers (2004–2008) to compare the productivity of full, associate, and assistant professors by discipline. *Relevance:* evidence that rank or career stage moderates output per person, supporting the career-stage adjustment. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11192-011-0426-6)

- **Fanelli, D., & Larivière, V. (2016). Researchers' individual publication rate has not increased in a century. *PLOS ONE*, 11(3), e0149504. doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0149504.** Verified: Crossref. *Summary:* Across more than 40,000 WoS researchers (1900–2013), total early-career papers rose, but fractionally counted (or first-author) productivity did not increase over the century in most disciplines. *Relevance:* supports a time-invariant fractional papers-per-researcher-year rate, which justifies applying one prior across 1980–2026 instead of inflating effort in recent decades. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0149504)

- **Ioannidis, J. P. A., Boyack, K. W., & Klavans, R. (2014). Estimates of the continuously publishing core in the scientific workforce. *PLOS ONE*, 9(7), e101698. doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0101698.** Verified: Crossref. *Summary:* Of 15.15 million Scopus author identifiers (1996–2011), only 150,608 (<1%) published every year. This core produced 41.7% of all papers and 87.1% of papers with more than 1,000 citations. *Relevance:* explains the long tail of one-paper "occasional" authors in our author map. It is a caution that headcount greatly overstates FTE and that the per-year floor matters. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0101698)

- **Larivière, V., & Costas, R. (2016). How many is too many? On the relationship between research productivity and impact. *PLOS ONE*, 11(9), e0162709. doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0162709.** Verified: Crossref. *Summary:* Over 28 million disambiguated researchers (1980–2013), authors with more papers have on average a higher share of top-1%-cited papers, with decreasing returns for recent cohorts. *Relevance:* context for the known bias that prolific authors get a smaller share per paper. Paper count is not a pure proxy for effort. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0162709)

**Career stage and productivity trajectories**

- **Way, S. F., Morgan, A. C., Clauset, A., & Larremore, D. B. (2017). The misleading narrative of the canonical faculty productivity trajectory. *PNAS*, 114(44), E9216–E9223. doi:10.1073/pnas.1702121114.** Verified: Crossref for authors, title, journal, volume, issue, and year. The page range is from memory and was not confirmed, because the Crossref record lacks pages. *Summary:* Using more than 200,000 publications by 2,453 tenure-track faculty in all 205 US/Canadian PhD-granting CS departments, the "rise to early peak then decline" pattern describes only about one fifth of faculty. Prestige predicts overall productivity. *Relevance:* a CS-specific source for career-stage productivity, and a caution that a single stage-based curve is a simplification. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1702121114)

- **Kyvik, S. (1990). Age and scientific productivity: differences between fields of learning. *Higher Education*, 19(1), 37–55. doi:10.1007/BF00142022.** Verified: Crossref and OpenAlex. *Summary:* A classic study of how publication productivity varies with age across fields (Norwegian faculty). Abstract not retrieved, so the summary is limited to the title. *Relevance:* a classic citation for field-dependent age and career-stage productivity. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF00142022)

**Time allocation (research fraction priors)**

- **OECD (2015). *Frascati Manual 2015: Guidelines for Collecting and Reporting Data on Research and Experimental Development*. The Measurement of Scientific, Technological and Innovation Activities. OECD Publishing, Paris. doi:10.1787/9789264239012-en.** Verified: Crossref. *Relevance:* the authoritative definition of R&D personnel in full-time equivalents (the share of working time spent on R&D), and of the use of time-use surveys to convert higher-education headcount into R&D FTE. This is the standard to cite for what "research-FTE" means. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1787/9789264239012-en)

- **Bentley, P. J., & Kyvik, S. (2012). Academic work from a comparative perspective: a survey of faculty working time across 13 countries. *Higher Education*, 63(4), 529–547. doi:10.1007/s10734-011-9457-4.** Verified: Crossref, plus the abstract via the HRK library catalogue. *Summary:* Uses survey data on how faculty at research universities in 13 countries split working time between tasks. Patterns differ significantly by country. Full professors are more alike across countries, with a stronger research orientation and more time on research than teaching. *Relevance:* the primary cross-national source for the "established academic ≈ 0.45 research fraction" prior. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10734-011-9457-4)

- **Link, A. N., Swann, C. A., & Bozeman, B. (2008). A time allocation study of university faculty. *Economics of Education Review*, 27(4), 363–374. doi:10.1016/j.econedurev.2007.04.002.** Verified: Crossref. *Summary (secondary, abstract not retrieved):* A survey of about 1,365 science and engineering faculty at 150 top US research universities (2004–05), analysing time on research, teaching, grant-writing, and service by tenure status and gender. Mean workweek reported as about 54 hours. *Relevance:* US research-university time-use evidence for the research-fraction priors. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.econedurev.2007.04.002). Sample and hours come from a [secondary summary](https://sauvonsluniversite.com/article6711.html) and are unverified.

- **Milem, J. F., Berger, J. B., & Dey, E. L. (2000). Faculty time allocation: a study of change over twenty years. *The Journal of Higher Education*, 71(4), 454–475. doi:10.1080/00221546.2000.11778845.** Verified: Crossref. *Summary:* Documents that US faculty across institution types spent increasing time on research and publication over two decades, with the size of the change depending on institution type. *Relevance:* supports time-varying and institution-dependent research fractions. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1080/00221546.2000.11778845)

### Inferences
- The study's model has the same structure as Abramo & D'Angelo's FSS: fractional credit, per year worked, with the input weighted. The difference is that we turn output share into a labour-share estimate. Citing FSS together with the Frascati FTE definition makes the "research-FTE" framing defensible. The repo's own caveat, that output share is not time share, is consistent with this literature, and no source validates output share as time share.
- The research-fraction priors (student 0.9, early career 0.85, established academic 0.45) fit the qualitative pattern in Bentley & Kyvik and Link et al., where faculty research time is well under half of total work time during teaching terms. The exact values are conventions and should be described as such.
- Fanelli & Larivière (2016) is the best single citation for applying one fractional papers-per-year rate across decades.

### Gaps
- **No canonical papers-per-FTE-year value for math or CS was found.** D'Angelo & Abramo (2015) is the source to open for MAT/INF fractional rates, but the numbers were not extracted (no access to the proceedings PDF). The 1–2 person-years-per-paper priors therefore lack a direct literature anchor and should be flagged as assumptions.
- Unverified figures: secondary sources attribute to Bentley & Kyvik mean weekly hours of about 15.7 h research out of 48.4 h in teaching terms and 23.9 of 44.7 h outside them. These came from one literature review that also cited the study inconsistently (as 2011, "over 7,000 faculty"). They are not used as findings. Check the original Table before quoting.
- No study was found that validates output share against measured time share at the individual level. This is a real methodological gap the paper should acknowledge.

## Q3. What is the evidence on LLM screening accuracy in systematic reviews (and LLM classification agreement with humans)?

### Takeaway
Validation studies (mostly biomedical) find that LLM title/abstract screeners can reach high sensitivity, roughly 90–99%, with carefully engineered prompts, but specificity and performance vary widely with model, prompt, and review topic. Zero-shot or naive prompts can miss half the relevant papers. Scoping reviews conclude that LLM screening is promising but not yet validated enough for unsupervised use. This supports the study's design (rubric-based prompts, two independent passes, kappa reporting, a false-negative audit, and planned human spot-checks) and argues against claiming accuracy without human validation.

### Cited Findings
- **Tran, V.-T., Gartlehner, G., Yaacoub, S., Boutron, I., Schwingshackl, L., Stadelmaier, J., Sommer, I., Alebouyeh, F., et al. (2024). Sensitivity and specificity of using GPT-3.5 Turbo models for title and abstract screening in systematic reviews and meta-analyses. *Annals of Internal Medicine*, 177(6), 791–799. doi:10.7326/M23-3389.** Verified: Crossref. *Summary:* 22,665 citations from 5 reviews. Under a balanced rule, sensitivity was 81.1–96.5% and specificity 25.8–80.4%. Under a sensitivity-optimized rule, sensitivity was 94.6–99.8% and specificity 2.2–46.6%. GPT found 7 of 708 includes that humans missed. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.7326/M23-3389)
- **Cao, C., Sang, J., Arora, R., Chen, D., Kloosterman, R., Cecere, M., Gorla, J., Saleh, R., et al. (2025). Development of prompt templates for large language model–driven screening in systematic reviews. *Annals of Internal Medicine*, 178(3), 389–401. doi:10.7326/ANNALS-24-02189.** Verified: Crossref. *Summary:* 48,425 citations across 10 reviews, with original full-text decisions as the reference standard. Optimized GPT-4 prompts reached weighted sensitivity of 97.7% and specificity of 85.2% for abstracts, and 96.5% / 91.2% for full text. Zero-shot prompts reached only about 49% sensitivity. Claude 3.5 and GPT-4 variants performed similarly. *Relevance:* the strongest evidence that structured, criterion-explicit prompts like our rubric are what make LLM screening work. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.7326/ANNALS-24-02189)
- **Oami, T., Okada, Y., & Nakada, T. (2024). Performance of a large language model in screening citations. *JAMA Network Open*, 7(7), e2420496. doi:10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2024.20496.** Verified: Crossref. *Summary:* A prospective study of GPT-4 Turbo screening for 5 sepsis-guideline questions (about 16.7k citations). Integrated sensitivity was 0.75 and specificity 0.99. A post hoc prompt revision raised sensitivity to 0.91. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2024.20496)
- **Guo, E., Gupta, M., Deng, J., Park, Y.-J., Paget, M., & Naugler, C. (2024). Automated paper screening for clinical reviews using large language models: data analysis study. *Journal of Medical Internet Research*, 26, e48996. doi:10.2196/48996.** Verified: Crossref. *Summary:* GPT/GPT-4 screening of more than 24,000 titles and abstracts across 6 reviews. Sensitivity was 0.91 for excluded and 0.76 for included papers. Inter-rater kappa between two humans was only 0.46, while the prevalence- and bias-adjusted kappa between the LLM and the human consensus was 0.96. *Relevance:* a useful benchmark showing that human-human agreement in screening is itself modest, which puts our kappa of 0.84–0.89 between passes in context. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.2196/48996)
- **Khraisha, Q., Put, S., Kappenberg, J., Warraitch, A., & Hadfield, K. (2024). Can large language models replace humans in systematic reviews? Evaluating GPT-4's efficacy in screening and extracting data from peer-reviewed and grey literature in multiple languages. *Research Synthesis Methods*, 15(4), 616–626. doi:10.1002/jrsm.1715.** Verified: Crossref. *Summary:* A pre-registered "human-out-of-the-loop" evaluation. GPT-4's raw accuracy looked human-like, but after adjusting for chance agreement and class imbalance, screening performance ranged from none to moderate. Full-text screening with highly reliable prompts reached human-like levels. *Relevance:* a caution that raw accuracy overstates performance on imbalanced corpora. Report kappa and recall, as the study does. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1002/jrsm.1715)
- **Dennstädt, F., Zink, J., Putora, P. M., Hastings, J., & Cihoric, N. (2024). Title and abstract screening for literature reviews using large language models: an exploratory study in the biomedical domain. *Systematic Reviews*, 13(1), 158. doi:10.1186/s13643-024-02575-4.** Verified: Crossref. *Summary:* Four open LLMs scored relevance on a Likert scale against ten published review datasets. Sensitivity/specificity ranged from 81.9%/75.2% (Mixtral) to 97.6%/19.1%, and results were sensitive to small prompt and scale changes. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13643-024-02575-4)
- **Delgado-Chaves, F. M., Jennings, M. J., Atalaia, A., Wolff, J., Horvath, R., Mamdouh, Z. M., Baumbach, J., & Baumbach, L. (2025). Transforming literature screening: the emerging role of large language models in systematic reviews. *PNAS*, 122(2), e2411962122. doi:10.1073/pnas.2411962122.** Verified: Crossref. *Summary:* Compares 18 LLMs with dual human screening on 3 reviews. LLMs could cut one reviewer's title/abstract workload by 33–93%, and the wording of the inclusion criteria strongly influenced performance. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2411962122)
- **Syriani, E., David, I., & Kumar, G. (2024). Screening articles for systematic reviews with ChatGPT. *Journal of Computer Languages*, 80, 101287. doi:10.1016/j.cola.2024.101287.** Verified: Crossref. *Summary:* Five software-engineering review datasets. ChatGPT reached up to 82% accuracy, and the best prompts state exclusion criteria explicitly and avoid negative examples. *Relevance:* a CS-domain (non-biomedical) validation, closer to our domain. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cola.2024.101287)
- **Lieberum, J.-L., Toews, M., Metzendorf, M.-I., Heilmeyer, F., Siemens, W., Haverkamp, C., Böhringer, D., Meerpohl, J. J., et al. (2025). Large language models for conducting systematic reviews: on the rise, but not yet ready for use—a scoping review. *Journal of Clinical Epidemiology*, 181, 111746. doi:10.1016/j.jclinepi.2025.111746.** Verified: Crossref. *Summary:* 37 articles. Study selection was the second most common LLM use (38%), and GPT was used in 89% of them. Only about half of the authors judged LLM use promising, and fully validated applications are lacking. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jclinepi.2025.111746)
- **Gilardi, F., Alizadeh, M., & Kubli, M. (2023). ChatGPT outperforms crowd workers for text-annotation tasks. *PNAS*, 120(30), e2305016120. doi:10.1073/pnas.2305016120.** Verified: Crossref. *Summary:* On 6,183 tweets and news articles, zero-shot ChatGPT beat MTurk crowd workers by about 25 percentage points in accuracy, and its intercoder agreement exceeded that of both crowd workers and trained annotators. It cost under $0.003 per annotation. *Relevance:* the most-cited general evidence for LLM text classification. 1,030 citations. — [Source](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2305016120)
- **O'Mara-Eves, A., Thomas, J., McNaught, J., Miwa, M., & Ananiadou, S. (2015). - **Landis, J. R., & Koch, G. G. (1977). The measurement of observer agreement for categorical data. *Biometrics*, 33(1), 159–174. doi:10.2307/2529310.** Verified: Crossref. *Relevance:* the standard citation for interpreting kappa. Our 0.84–0.89 falls in their "almost perfect" band (>0.80). — [Source](https://doi.org/10.2307/2529310)

### Inferences
- Across studies, prompt design and explicit criteria matter more than the choice of model (Cao et al.; Delgado-Chaves et al.; Syriani et al.). Our versioned rubric fits this evidence.
- Agreement between two LLM passes is not validity: both passes share the model's biases. The literature's reference standard is human (ideally full-text) decisions, so the planned human spot-check sheet is necessary before claiming accuracy.
- Human-human screening kappa can be modest (0.46 in Guo et al.), so LLM–human disagreement should be judged against a human–human baseline.

### Gaps
- Nearly all validation studies are biomedical. Syriani et al. is the only CS-domain study found, and none is in cryptography or mathematics.
- A JMAI 2025 systematic review reporting pooled sensitivity and specificity near 90% across 14 LLM models, and a medRxiv meta-analysis, appeared in search results but were not verified. They are omitted.

## Q4. What supports the bibliographic coverage and author-disambiguation choices (DBLP, OpenAlex, zbMATH, snowballing)?

### Takeaway
DBLP's author disambiguation is well evaluated (pairwise F1 around 0.90 or above; weakest on distinct people with identical names), and DBLP is documented by its founder. Coverage studies show that no single database is complete for CS: in one university sample, DBLP held about 61% of CS journal articles against 76% for Scopus, with each source containing unique items. OpenAlex is broadly comparable to or a superset of Scopus but has metadata-quality issues, including poor conference venue data. This supports the study's multi-source design and its zbMATH supplement for mathematicians.

### Inferences
- The recall benchmark against seed-paper reference lists (96–100%) is a "known-item" check in the spirit of these coverage studies. Citing Visser et al. and Cavacini frames why a multi-source union was needed.
- Kim (2018) shows DBLP's main failure mode is merging different people with identical names. That is the opposite of the repo's main known bias (one person split into several string identities), so the two error types should be discussed separately.

### Gaps
- No published study directly benchmarks DBLP against OpenAlex against zbMATH coverage for cryptography or mathematics venues. The 2024 arXiv gender-citation study (arXiv:2408.01900) notes that OpenAlex conference names are often missing or undisambiguated for CS papers. This came from a search snippet and was not opened.
- zbMATH Open documentation: Petrera et al., "zbMATH Open: API solutions and research challenges" (arXiv:2106.04664, 2021) was found in OpenAlex but not opened. It is listed as unverified, though it is likely the right citation for the zbMATH API.
- Franceschet (2010), "A comparison of bibliometric indicators for computer science scholars and journals on Web of Science and Google Scholar," *Scientometrics* 83(1):243–258, doi:10.1007/s11192-009-0021-2, was verified in Crossref but its abstract was not read. It is optional and relevant to CS conference coverage.
