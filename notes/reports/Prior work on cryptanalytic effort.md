# Prior work counts breaks, not cryptanalytic effort

No prior work we could find measures, on a common basis across assumption classes, how much public cryptanalysis ECDLP, lattices, syndrome decoding, factoring, isogenies and multivariate problems have each received. The study's central measurement appears to be new. The surrounding literature is large but stops short in a consistent way. Standards bodies and critics agree that confidence in an assumption comes from attacks that have failed over time, but they judge the amount of analysis qualitatively. Quantitative prior work counts outcomes (which submissions broke), curates paper lists for one assumption, runs bibliometrics on one attack technique, or models progress rates. The two closest precedents are Daniel J. Bernstein's 2023 risk paper, which counts breaks among NIST PQC submissions and names cryptanalytic attention as an untested hypothesis, and NISTIR 7896, in which NIST considered counting cryptanalysis papers per SHA-3 finalist and rejected the idea as "rather misleading." The study therefore has to cite these as precedents and answer NIST's objections directly. Its other two components are also unprecedented as a combination: no published comparison puts post-quantum record computations next to factoring and ECDLP on one gap-to-frontier scale, and no published study converts the human literature on a specific problem into an AI inference budget. Several citation errors and unverifiable figures in the study's own materials need fixing before publication; they are listed near the end.

## Verdict: nothing pre-empts the study, and Bernstein comes closest

The verdict rests on six searches across standards documents, cryptanalysis surveys, scientometrics journals, record literature and AI-economics preprints. None found a per-assumption effort measurement. The closest work, ranked by how much of the study it anticipates, is in the table below.

| Rank | Precedent | What it quantifies | Why it does not pre-empt |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Bernstein, "Quantifying risks in cryptographic selection processes" (2023) | Break rates among NIST PQC submissions: 33 of 69 round-1 (about 48%), 12 of 48 round-1 survivors (25%), 10 of 28 round-2 (about 36%) | Measures outcomes per submission, not effort per assumption; lists "NIST selection draws more cryptanalytic attention" as an untested "Hypothesis 6" ([ePrint 2023/1838](https://eprint.iacr.org/2023/1838)) |
| 2 | NISTIR 7896, SHA-3 third-round report (2012) | Considered paper counts per finalist, then used a subjective "depth of cryptanalysis" rating | Rejected counting as "rather misleading"; symmetric primitives only ([NIST](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/ir/2012/NIST.IR.7896.pdf)) |
| 3 | Bernstein, "Cryptographic Competitions" (J. Cryptology 2024) | Survival per competition phase; a p(M) model of break probability after M months; an argument that cryptanalyst throughput is the binding constraint | Poses the effort question without measuring it ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00145-023-09467-1)) |
| 4 | Zheng & Kang, lattice cryptanalysis of RSA (2024) | 400 Scopus papers (1996–2023) and 61 core authors on one attack technique | One technique, one family, no FTE conversion ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1186/s42400-024-00289-7)) |
| 5 | Classic McEliece design rationale (2022) | A curated list of about 30 post-1978 attack papers on one assumption | Submitter-curated, one assumption, used rhetorically ([PDF](https://classic.mceliece.org/mceliece-rationale-20221023.pdf)) |
| 6 | Gupta & Rana, GAUGE (arXiv preprint, September 2026) | Survival analysis of time-to-break for 22 assumption "generations" | Records birth year and break year only, with no effort or paper counts; unrefereed and concurrent ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.17281)) |
| 7 | NESSIE comments on the AES finalists (2000) | A single person-year figure: IBM spent "17 man-years" on DES | Anecdote with no stated source ([Wayback PDF](https://web.archive.org/web/2005/https://www.cosic.esat.kuleuven.be/nessie/deliverables/D4_NessieAESInput.pdf)) |
| 8 | Moody, NIST PQC round-2 slides (2019) | Distribution of official comments: about 60% of more than 300 concerned roughly 10 submissions | Measures attention by comment count, within one competition ([NIST slides](https://csrc.nist.gov/csrc/media/presentations/round-2-of-the-nist-pqc-competition-what-was-nist/images-media/pqcrypto-may2019-moody.pdf)) |

Two adjacent efforts deserve mention as alternative methods rather than precedents. Van de Graaf and Lenstra built "Delphi," an online questionnaire to collect researchers' beliefs about assumption strength, though no aggregated results could be found ([ePrint 2024/157](https://eprint.iacr.org/2024/157)). Brown models attack discovery as a Poisson process to turn effort into assurance, but he does not measure effort ([ePrint 2019/1465](https://eprint.iacr.org/2019/1465)). For the frontier-gap component, the nearest single analogue is Bos, Kaihara, Kleinjung, Lenstra and Montgomery's 2009 risk assessment of RSA-1024 and 160-bit ECC from the latest record computations, a two-family, classical-only "distance from records" analysis ([ePrint 2009/389](https://eprint.iacr.org/2009/389)). For the AI component, the nearest precedents are aggregate. MacAskill and Moorhouse measure AI research effort in "remote human researcher" equivalents across the economy ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.14863)), and Anthropic informally contrasts HAWK's two years of expert review with 60 hours of model work ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/research/discovering-cryptographic-weaknesses)).

The coverage has limits that the claim should reflect. dblp blocked automated access, most iacr.org PDFs returned HTTP 403, and Springer blocked the full texts of the two 2025 PQC bibliometric studies, so a per-family table inside Hasija et al. or Hanafi and Ali cannot be ruled out. The absence finding should therefore be phrased as a statement of awareness. A safe wording for the paper and website is:

> *We are not aware of prior work that measures the cumulative volume of public cryptanalysis directed at each public-key hardness assumption on a common basis. Prior quantitative work counts outcomes, such as which submissions were broken (Bernstein 2023; Gupta and Rana 2026), counts papers for a single assumption or attack technique (Classic McEliece 2022; Zheng and Kang 2024), or models rates of cryptanalytic progress (Lenstra and Verheul 2001). NIST weighs the "quantity, quality, and maturity of analysis" qualitatively, and in the SHA-3 competition explicitly declined to count cryptanalysis papers (NISTIR 7896). We are likewise not aware of a published comparison that places lattice and code-based record computations alongside factoring and ECDLP records on a single gap-to-frontier scale, or of prior work converting the accumulated human literature on a specific problem into an AI inference budget.*

## NIST weighs "quantity of analysis" but has never measured it

Every NIST post-quantum report treats scrutiny as the basis of confidence, and none counts it. NISTIR 8105 (2016) said post-quantum proposals "have not received nearly as much scrutiny" as deployed algorithms, that isogenies had had too little analysis for much confidence, and set the schedule to allow "3 to 5 years of public scrutiny." That figure is a process timetable, not a sufficiency threshold ([NISTIR 8105](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/ir/2016/NIST.IR.8105.pdf)). The round-1 report says NIST weighed "the overall quantity, quality, and maturity of analysis" ([NISTIR 8240](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/ir/2019/NIST.IR.8240.pdf)), and the same wording recurs in the additional-signature reports ([NIST IR 8528](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/ir/2024/NIST.IR.8528.pdf); [NIST IR 8610](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/ir/2026/NIST.IR.8610.pdf)). A full-text search of each status report found no tabulation of papers or effort per candidate. The vocabulary is entirely calendar-based: NTRU "scrutinized for decades," MLWE "a relatively young problem" ([NISTIR 8309](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/ir/2020/NIST.IR.8309.pdf)), Kyber "thoroughly analyzed" and SIKE "relatively new" ([NIST IR 8413-upd1](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/ir/2022/NIST.IR.8413-upd1.pdf)). NIST's only numeric attention measures are comment counts: 136 sets of public comments in AES round 2 ([Nechvatal et al. 2001](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/jres/106/3/j63nec.pdf)) and more than 300 in PQC round 1, with **about half of the 69 submissions receiving two or fewer** ([Moody 2019](https://csrc.nist.gov/csrc/media/presentations/round-2-of-the-nist-pqc-competition-what-was-nist/images-media/pqcrypto-may2019-moody.pdf)).

The one time NIST considered counting papers, it said no. In the SHA-3 final report, NIST called paper counting easy but "rather misleading" and gave three reasons. Tweaked finalists attract papers that re-apply old attacks. Some algorithms attract many small results rather than a few deep ones. Weaker algorithms may attract more attack papers. NIST relied instead on a subjective "depth of cryptanalysis" rating, judging that Grøstl, Skein and BLAKE received more analysis than Keccak and JH much less ([NISTIR 7896](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/ir/2012/NIST.IR.7896.pdf)). This is the single most important precedent for the study, because it is a documented objection by the relevant standards body to exactly the study's measurement. It also offers a validation target: a paper count on the SHA-3 finalists could be checked against NIST's ordinal judgements. The lightweight-cryptography reports follow the same qualitative pattern, describing ASCON as "the submission with the most third-party analysis" without a count ([NIST IR 8454](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/ir/2023/NIST.IR.8454.pdf)).

Bernstein supplies the sharpest critique and the closest framing. His 2023 risk paper notes that NIST's claim to weigh the "overall quantity" of analysis could be made measurable, for example by counting pages of security analysis, but has not been ([qrcsp PDF](https://cr.yp.to/papers/qrcsp-20231202.pdf)). His competitions paper argues that public cryptanalysis is throughput-limited by a small pool of experts, quotes the NESSIE comparison of "very limited" AES-finalist evaluation with 17 IBM man-years on DES, and estimates p(12) ≈ 1/3 from 5 of 15 AES submissions broken within a year ([J. Cryptology 2024](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00145-023-09467-1)). The study's premise, that scrutiny is a scarce and measurable resource, is stated there in almost those words.

The maturity debate also uses three distinct metrics that are often conflated. The first is calendar age (NIST's "decades"). The second is volume of attack work: Bernstein and Lange's "dozens" of information-set-decoding papers ([Nature 2017](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature23461)) and the Classic McEliece paper list. The third is stability of the best-attack exponent, which Bernstein says is the core argument for McEliece, "not just its age, and not just the number of papers" ([blog 2025-04-23](https://blog.cr.yp.to/20250423-mceliece.html)). **The study measures the second**, and should say so. That matters because the live dispute between NIST and Bernstein is about the third. NIST IR 8545 says recent algebraic and distinguisher work "somewhat weaken[s]" McEliece's long-history argument ([NIST IR 8545](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/ir/2025/NIST.IR.8545.pdf)). Bernstein replies that lattice estimates keep eroding while McEliece's do not, and in 2026 asked whether ML-KEM dimensions will look as naive in 15 years as FrodoKEM's original ones do now ([blog 2026-06-30](https://blog.cr.yp.to/20260630-risk.html)). The NTRU Prime team's qualitative per-KEM table of "attack avenues not ruled out by theorems" is the most detailed scheme-level risk taxonomy, but it is not an effort measure ([warnings page](https://ntruprime.cr.yp.to/warnings.html)).

The Koblitz–Menezes line makes the complementary historical point: confidence requires long study of natural problems. Their "bodacious assumptions" essay contrasts the expectation that proofs rest on a few well-studied problems with a "menagerie" of unusual ones ([authors' page](https://math.uwaterloo.ca/~ajmeneze/anotherlook/ams2.shtml)). Their history of ECC records **about sixteen years** from mistrust to near-unquestioned acceptance, which is the main case study for how long ECDLP took to earn trust ([JNT 2011](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jnt.2009.01.006)). Both were read at abstract level only. Outside NIST, ANSSI says PQC "lack[s] cryptanalytical hindsight" and mandates hybrids ([ANSSI 2022](https://messervices.cyber.gouv.fr/guides/en-anssi-views-post-quantum-cryptography-transition)).

The breaks literature shows why calendar age is a weak proxy, which is the study's strongest motivating argument. SIDH ran about 11 years from proposal ([Jao and De Feo 2011](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-642-25405-5_2)) to a ten-minute single-core key recovery ([Castryck and Decru 2023](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-031-30589-4_15)). Costello's "Case for SIKE," a decade-of-scrutiny defence with $55k in challenge prizes, appeared about a year before the break ([ePrint 2021/543](https://eprint.iacr.org/2021/543)). Rainbow ran about 17 years from proposal ([Ding and Schmidt 2005](https://doi.org/10.1007/11496137_12)) to a 53-hour laptop key recovery ([Beullens 2022](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-031-15979-4_16)). The sources show only duration and break. Attributing these breaks to a small attacker pool is an inference the study's own data could test.

## Theory treats scrutiny as a precondition, never as a quantity

The theory of cryptographic assumptions makes falsifiability, not amount of study, its organising principle, and it explicitly sets the amount of study aside. Naor proposed classifying assumptions by how hard it is to falsify them through a public challenge, which is the founding statement that confidence comes from exposure to refutation ([CRYPTO 2003](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-540-45146-4_6)). Gentry and Wichs formalised falsifiable assumptions as games with efficient challengers ([STOC 2011](https://doi.org/10.1145/1993636.1993651)). Goldwasser and Kalai's position paper is the key citation for the study. It calls any new assumption "an open invitation to refute or simplify," and **states that its classification omits robustness measures such as how well studied an assumption is**, while listing factoring, RSA, DL, LPN and LWE together as traditional well-studied assumptions ([TCC 2016-A](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-662-49096-9_21)). The study measures exactly the axis that the leading theory paper leaves outside its framework. No theory paper found states a minimum period or amount of scrutiny.

Lattice theorists have conceded the depth-of-study point in their own words. Peikert's survey says ideal lattices have not been studied nearly as deeply computationally, so their hardness conjectures "may not yet deserve as much confidence" ([Peikert 2016](https://doi.org/10.1561/0400000074)). That supports the study's split of structured from plain lattices. His 2015 post on the Soliloquy break argues that many experts' failed attempts on Ideal-SVP count as evidence, while conceding that a weakness went unnoticed for years ([blog](https://web.eecs.umich.edu/~cpeikert/soliloquy.html)). Crockett and Peikert published Ring-LWE challenges because lattice cryptography needed "further cryptanalytic effort" ([ePrint 2016/782](https://eprint.iacr.org/2016/782)), putting Naor's challenge idea into practice.

## Cryptography bibliometrics stop at the topic level

Scientometric work on cryptography exists but never separates attacks from constructions by assumption. Pal profiled the Journal of Cryptology for 2001–2010 (2.4 authors per paper, partial fit to Lotka's law) and found "cryptanalysis" only as a frequent keyword ([Scientometrics 2015](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11192-015-1661-z)). Biryukov and Dong used DBLP to compare computer-science communities and found the CRYPTO community has long careers, small teams and weakly clustered co-author networks ([TPDL 2010](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-642-15464-5_24)). That supports the study's author-fractional credit, since small teams make fractional and whole counts diverge less. The 2025 PQC bibliometrics show why keyword queries fail. Hasija et al.'s 14,230 Scopus "PQC" records list a quantum-key-distribution pioneer as the most-cited author ([J. Big Data](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40537-025-01269-5)), and Hanafi and Ali's 2,874 articles are mapped by topic, not attack target ([Discover Computing](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10791-025-09507-3)). Zheng and Kang's single-technique study is the only precedent for counting attack papers and core authors, and it can serve as a cross-check on part of the study's factoring class ([Cybersecurity 2024](https://doi.org/10.1186/s42400-024-00289-7)). Symmetric cryptography has curated lists but no effort estimates. The Keccak team's third-party list holds about 90 results over 2009–2026 by the note-taker's count ([keccak.team](https://keccak.team/third_party.html)), and Bernstein's eSTREAM tracker records per-cipher status ([cr.yp.to](https://cr.yp.to/streamciphers/attacks.html)). For scale, the study's provisional ECDLP class alone holds about 465 fractional papers.

The effort-conversion step has solid methodological anchors but no direct calibration. Author-level fractional counting is the recommended standard ([Waltman and van Eck 2015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joi.2015.08.001)), with terminology from Gauffriau et al. ([2007](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11192-007-1800-2)) and a modified-fractional alternative for sensitivity analysis ([Sivertsen et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joi.2019.03.010)). Abramo and D'Angelo's Fractional Scientific Strength, fractional output per year worked weighted by salary, has the same structure as the study's model run in reverse ([Scientometrics 2014](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11192-014-1269-8)). The OECD Frascati Manual defines research FTE as the share of working time spent on R&D ([OECD 2015](https://doi.org/10.1787/9789264239012-en)), and faculty time-use surveys put research well under half of working time during teaching terms ([Bentley and Kyvik 2012](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10734-011-9457-4); [Link et al. 2008](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.econedurev.2007.04.002)). Fanelli and Larivière found that fractionally counted productivity per researcher has not risen in a century in most disciplines, which justifies one rate across 1980–2026 ([PLOS ONE 2016](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0149504)). Ioannidis et al. found that **fewer than 1% of Scopus authors publish every year, yet they produce 41.7% of papers** ([PLOS ONE 2014](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0101698)), so headcount greatly overstates FTE. **No canonical papers-per-FTE-year value for mathematics or computer science was found.** D'Angelo and Abramo's 192-field publication rates are the source to extract ([CNR record](https://iris.cnr.it/handle/20.500.14243/351686)), but the numbers were not obtained. The study's 1–2 person-years-per-paper priors must be presented as assumptions.

Source coverage and disambiguation are well studied. DBLP's disambiguation reaches pairwise F1 around 0.90 and fails mainly by merging different people with the same name ([Kim 2018](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11192-018-2824-5)). That is the opposite error to the study's known bias, which splits one person into several string identities. DBLP held only 61% of one university's CS journal articles against 76% for Scopus ([Cavacini 2015](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11192-014-1506-1)). OpenAlex is broadly a superset of Scopus with metadata-quality issues ([Alperin et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2404.17663)), and its reference coverage matches WoS and Scopus ([Culbert et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11192-025-05293-3)). Together these justify the multi-source union and the zbMATH supplement, whose author profiles are manually curated ([Müller et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11192-017-2363-5)).

LLM screening is validated mainly in medicine, and the evidence favours the study's design while warning against its current claims. Criterion-explicit GPT-4 prompts reached **97.7% sensitivity and 85.2% specificity** on 48,425 citations, while zero-shot prompts reached only about 49% sensitivity ([Cao et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.7326/ANNALS-24-02189)). That supports a versioned rubric. Human–human screening kappa can be as low as 0.46 ([Guo et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.2196/48996)), which puts the study's inter-pass kappa of 0.84–0.89 in the "almost perfect" band ([Landis and Koch 1977](https://doi.org/10.2307/2529310)). But raw accuracy overstates performance on imbalanced corpora ([Khraisha et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1002/jrsm.1715)), a 2025 scoping review found no fully validated applications ([Lieberum et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jclinepi.2025.111746)), and the only computer-science validation found is in software engineering ([Syriani et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cola.2024.101287)). None is in cryptography or mathematics.

## Record trackers price deployed targets but never the gap to the frontier

Record-based extrapolation is a mature, Lenstra-led tradition, but it outputs equivalent key sizes or attack costs and covers only classical families. Lenstra and Verheul derived key sizes for symmetric ciphers, RSA, finite-field DL and ECC from explicit hypotheses applied "uniform[ly and] mechanical[ly]" to record data. Their default assumption was that cryptanalytic progress halves classical public-key attack cost every 18 months, with both "no progress" and Moore-rate scenarios for elliptic curves ([J. Cryptology 2001](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00145-001-0009-4); [summary](https://www.cs.ru.nl/E.Verheul/papers/cce1999/CCE1999.pdf)). Lenstra's 2004 update adds "double Moore" (factoring cost halves every 9 months) and "triple Moore" laws ([keylength.com](https://www.keylength.com/en/2/)). Universal currencies, energy ([Lenstra, Kleinjung and Thomé 2013](https://eprint.iacr.org/2013/635)) and cloud dollars ([Kleinjung et al. 2012](https://eprint.iacr.org/2011/254)), price the attack itself rather than its distance from public capability. The models disagree mainly on RSA: at 128-bit security, recommended moduli range from 3072 to 5888 bits ([ECRYPT-CSA 2018](https://web.archive.org/web/2023id_/https://www.ecrypt.eu.org/csa/documents/D5.4-FinalAlgKeySizeProt.pdf)). Smart and Thomé's history of key sizes is the best single survey for a related-work paragraph ([CUP 2021](https://doi.org/10.1017/9781108854207.014)).

Individual record papers extrapolate to deployed sizes within their own family, and the study's leading GNFS model reproduces them. The RSA-768 authors called 1024-bit "about a thousand times harder" ([Kleinjung et al. 2010](https://ir.cwi.nl/pub/16870/16870D.pdf)). The study's formula gives 10.25 bits, about 1,220×. Boudot et al. found the L(1/3) prediction of 2.25× for DLP-240 over DLP-768 matches the formula's 1.17 bits, but the measured relation collection was cheaper than the older record. They called the timeline of previous records "misleading" ([CRYPTO 2020](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10273009)). Esser, May and Zweydinger extrapolated their ISD records to find Classic McEliece's 192- and 256-bit sets overestimated by roughly 20 and 10 bits ([ePrint 2021/1634](https://eprint.iacr.org/2021/1634)). The estimators (Lattice Estimator, SD Estimator, CryptographicEstimators) all price the deployed target but never the record instance in the same model ([Albrecht et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1515/jmc-2015-0016); [Esser and Bellini 2022](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-030-97121-2_5); [Esser et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1145/3634737.3645007)). That supports the study's choice to put both endpoints into one model by hand.

Progress-rate models exist only for factoring, and they were too optimistic. Brent fitted Y = 13.24·D^(1/3) + 1928.6, predicting 1024-bit general factoring in 2018 ([Brent 2000](https://maths-people.anu.edu.au/brent/pd/rpb196sp.pdf)). Against the study's records, that line puts RSA-896 around 2014 versus the actual 2026, so public factoring has run roughly a decade behind it. No progress-rate fit exists for SVP-challenge or decoding-challenge records, so the study's planned cross-family "gap shrinkage" tracking would be the first. The unrefereed GAUGE preprint derives a lattice-sieving "drift" of about 9.79 bits per year from the sieving exponent falling from 0.415 to 0.292 over 2008–2016 ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.17281)). This is a possible comparator but needs independent checking.

## AI comparisons price tasks, not accumulated literatures

The per-problem conversion of human literature into an inference budget appears new. The economics precedent for pricing research input at a wage is Bloom, Jones, Van Reenen and Webb, who measure research effort as R&D spending deflated by high-skill wages ([AER 2020](https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.20180338)). MacAskill and Moorhouse supply the "human-researcher equivalent" unit at economy scale ([arXiv 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.14863)). OpenAI's only published per-researcher figure, a median of more than $600 per day of inference, scales to about $150k per year, the same order as the study's $250k per research-year ([OpenAI 2026](https://openai.com/index/research-acceleration-view-inside-openai/)).

Per-task comparisons bracket the study's 1×/10×/100× scenarios, and they show that the accounting boundary dominates. Counting only successful runs, METR found more than 80% cost under 10% of a human engineer's wage ([Kwa et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.14499)). GDPval reports GPT-5 as **474× cheaper than experts naively, but only 1.18–1.63× cheaper once human review and redo are counted** ([Patwardhan et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.04374)). An RCT found AI tools slowed experienced developers by 19% ([Becker et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.09089)), and RE-Bench humans outscore agents at long time budgets ([Wijk et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.15114)). Cost-of-Pass formalises expected dollars per correct solution as per-attempt cost divided by success rate ([Erol et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.13359)), the same construction as Epoch's roughly $10,000 expected cost per Erdős resolution ([Adamczewski and Bloom 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.25050)). None of these sources involves research-paper-sized mathematics or cryptanalysis.

The open-problem literature supplies the study's best justification for counting papers rather than years. Tao's Erdős-problems wiki warns that "absence of past progress may reflect obscurity rather than difficulty," and treats partial-result literature as stronger evidence of difficulty ([disclaimers](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/wiki/teorth/erdosproblems/Disclaimers-and-caveats.md)). Feng et al. found that 8 of 13 "open" Erdős problems Gemini addressed were already solved in the literature ([arXiv 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.22401)). Two labs decline to equate AI dollars with human labour. DeepMind's paper states it does not intend USD as a comparison of AI and human mathematical labour ([Tsoukalas et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.22763v2)), and Epoch gives no human baseline. The study's framing as "conditional budget comparison, not exchange rate" matches that caution. In cryptanalysis specifically, pre-2026 machine-learning attacks (Gohr's neural distinguishers; the SALSA line on LWE) were narrow and made no human-effort comparison. SALSA PICANTE states that it "does not threaten NIST's proposed LWE standards" ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.04178)). The 2026 HAWK case is the single in-domain anchor ([Straznickas and Weis](https://eprint.iacr.org/2026/1593)), and an independent ePrint confirms that the AI-derived results preceded the scheme's withdrawal ([Luo, Jiang and Pan](https://eprint.iacr.org/2026/2198)).

## Methodological caveats the study must answer

**NISTIR 7896's three biases.** NIST's objections to paper counting map onto concrete checks ([NISTIR 7896](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/ir/2012/NIST.IR.7896.pdf)). Re-application papers on tweaked variants inflate counts; the rubric's handling of variant-specific and re-application papers should be stated. Many shallow results versus few deep ones should be answered with author-FTE and record cross-checks, and ideally with a depth-weighted sensitivity run. "Weaker targets attract more papers" is a reverse-causation risk that matters most for multivariate and isogenies, where breaks generated follow-up literature. Post-break papers should be reported separately. Running the method on the SHA-3 finalists and comparing with NIST's ordinal judgements would turn the objection into a validation test.

**Volume is not stability.** Bernstein separates age, paper count and exponent stability ([blog 2025-04-23](https://blog.cr.yp.to/20250423-mceliece.html)). The study should say it measures volume, and that volume does not settle the NIST–Bernstein dispute over McEliece. NIST's language on McEliece shifted from long history that "inspires" confidence (8309) to "somewhat weaken[s]" (8545).

**Visible effort is a lower bound.** Failed attacks go unpublished, as Tao's disclaimer 3 notes for mathematics, and classified effort is invisible. The counts measure published scrutiny, not total scrutiny.

**LLM classification validity.** Agreement between two passes of the same model is not validity, because both passes share the model's biases. The literature's reference standard is human, ideally full-text, decisions ([Cao et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.7326/ANNALS-24-02189)). The planned human spot-check is necessary before any accuracy claim. Results should be reported as kappa and recall rather than raw accuracy ([Khraisha et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1002/jrsm.1715)) and compared against a human–human baseline.

**Output share is not time share.** No study validates fractional output share against measured time share at the individual level. The 1–2 person-years-per-paper priors and the 0.9/0.85/0.45 research fractions are conventions consistent with time-use surveys, not literature values. Prolific authors' smaller per-paper shares ([Larivière and Costas 2016](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0162709)) and the long tail of occasional authors ([Ioannidis et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0101698)) should be discussed.

**Disambiguation error types.** DBLP over-merges homonyms ([Kim 2018](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11192-018-2824-5)), while string-identity fallbacks over-split. The two biases push headcounts in opposite directions and should be discussed separately.

**NFS o(1) extrapolation.** The o(1) term in the NFS complexity converges extremely slowly, apparently only for N > exp(exp(25)), which "raises doubts" about estimates that set it to zero ([Le Gluher, Spaenlehauer and Thomé](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.02730)). The leading model reproduces short jumps such as 768→1024, but the 896→2048 gap is far longer and must carry this caveat. Thomé has also said publicly that rigorous extrapolation from records is "quite hard" ([NIST 2024](https://csrc.nist.gov/presentations/2024/crclub-2024-03-20)). The G6K authors call their own timing fits unreliable for extrapolation, and Boudot et al. warn that record timelines mislead.

**Failure accounting in AI disclosures.** Tao notes "severe reporting bias — successes are announced and failures are not," and that attempts, scaffolding and compute often go undisclosed ([Tao 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.16753)). Epoch's additional non-benchmark attempts cost "over $220,000," against about $20,000 for the benchmark run ([Adamczewski and Bloom](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.25050)). DeepMind says its reported costs "do not capture the full cost of discovery." Anthropic's ~$100k is presented per result, without saying whether failed earlier campaigns are included. Human validation (two researchers for nearly a month on HAWK; several hundred hours on AES) is excluded ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/research/discovering-cryptographic-weaknesses)). GDPval's two-orders-of-magnitude swing shows that this boundary choice matters more than the model choice.

**Neglect versus difficulty.** AI successes may cluster on low-scrutiny problems ([Feng et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.22401)). This is the study's motivation, but it also means AI cost-per-result figures from obscure problems do not transfer to heavily studied assumptions.

## Conclusion

The literature's working epistemology is that confidence equals survived attack effort over time, discounted for structure. Every element of that formula has been argued about for two decades except the effort term, which has never been measured. The study's contribution is therefore not just a new number but the first test of whether the folk ordering of assumption maturity holds up in volume terms. Its provisional result partly reverses that ordering. Lattices are calendar-young but already exceed ECDLP in cumulative visible effort (1.3–2.6×, all since 2016). Codes are calendar-old but have received less effort than ECDLP (0.6–0.8×). That reversal is exactly what NIST's "decades" vocabulary and Bernstein's stability arguments cannot capture, and it gives both sides of the McEliece-versus-lattices debate something new to argue with.

The biggest risk to the study's reception is not novelty but legitimacy. NIST has already gone on record against paper counting, and AI cost disclosures are self-reported and success-filtered. The study is strongest if it engages NISTIR 7896 head-on (ideally by running the SHA-3 validation), states that it measures volume rather than stability, and presents both the FTE conversion and the AI budgets as conditional scenarios. Bernstein's "Hypothesis 6", that selection attracts attention, is directly testable with the study's time series, and that test would be a natural headline result.
